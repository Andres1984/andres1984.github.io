Research Agenda

How are asset prices formed when information is incomplete, noisy, and shaped by investor behavior?

My research agenda develops this question through a narrative that connects asset valuation, behavioral finance, Bayesian statistics, and data science. Rather than studying prices as purely mechanical outcomes, I examine how they emerge from the interaction between information, expectations, market structure, and uncertainty.

This page is designed as a visual and conceptual story. Each section can be connected to animations that show how signals enter the market, how investors react, how submarkets emerge, and how prices are formed through the interaction between signal, bias, volatility, and noise.

The Story of Price Formation

01

Market Price: A Language with Its Own Grammar

Market prices are often treated as simple numbers. But they can also be understood as a language that encodes information, expectations, and the interaction of investors interpreting the same reality in different ways.

Imagine a person who wants to buy a stock for the first time in the financial market. Before doing so, several questions arise, but most of them revolve around two fundamental concerns. First, how much should I pay, and when is the best time to buy? If the only observable feature of that asset is its current market price, it quickly becomes clear that this information alone is not enough. Something more is needed to understand why that asset is priced the way it is at a given moment.

This is one of the most interesting problems in finance, particularly in asset pricing. Understanding the value of an asset and determining how much one should pay for it is a task that occupies both professional and amateur investors. Through their decisions, they generate price movements that reflect an imperfect consensus about the value of the asset.

In this sense, market prices can be understood as a language. Each transaction, a buy or a sell, is the result of an interpretation, ranging from simple intuition to sophisticated models. Learning to invest, then, is not only about choosing correctly, but about understanding this language.

But like any language, understanding it requires grammar. Only by learning this grammar can we interpret what the market is saying and express our own views through investment decisions. The difference between gains and losses often lies not in the information itself, but in how well we understand and participate in this language.

The grammar of market prices can be understood through three elements. The first is fundamentals, which is the ability of an asset to generate value over time. The second is expectations, which emerge from how information is interpreted and how signals propagate across investors. The third is noise, which reflects the diversity of opinions that gives liquidity and movement to the market.

Together, these elements shape how prices evolve. But more importantly, they determine how different investors interpret the same signal in different ways.

Ultimately, investing is not about finding the correct price. It is about learning to read, interpret, and participate in a language that is constantly being rewritten.

02

Investors do not react in the same way

Once information enters the market, investors process it through expectations, beliefs, strategic behavior, and emotional responses. This is where behavioral finance becomes central.

Herding, information cascades, and speculative reactions can amplify trends and reshape market dynamics. Instead of assuming homogeneous agents, I study how heterogeneous reactions help create submarkets, local biases, and collective movements.

03

Prices emerge from signal, structure, and noise

Prices are not only reflections of fundamentals. They are also shaped by uncertainty, volatility, and the way investors coordinate their actions.

In this sense, noise is not simply a disturbance; it is part of the market process itself. A key objective of my work is to understand how market prices emerge from the interaction between meaningful signals, behavioral biases, and stochastic fluctuations.

04

Uncertainty requires probabilistic models

Because markets are uncertain, the modeling framework must represent uncertainty explicitly. This is why Bayesian statistics plays a central role in my research.

Bayesian methods allow the integration of prior knowledge, observed data, and latent market structures in a coherent way. Through hierarchical models, stochastic volatility frameworks, and Bayesian learning, I study how risk, heterogeneity, and information interact across assets and over time.

05

Data science helps reveal hidden structure

Data science complements financial theory by providing tools to detect nonlinear patterns, classify structures, and build predictive systems.

In my research, machine learning is not used as a substitute for economic reasoning, but as a bridge between theory, evidence, and decision-making. This allows me to connect pricing models with visual narratives that explain how markets learn, differentiate signal from noise, and generate observable price paths.

Methods and Foundations

Research Foundations

My work is grounded in asset valuation, behavioral finance, stochastic modeling, and Bayesian inference. Across these areas, I aim to build models that remain economically meaningful while being statistically rigorous.

Methodological Toolkit

Bayesian hierarchical models, stochastic volatility, risk-factor models, market microstructure, machine learning, data visualization, and computational methods for uncertainty quantification.
