Research Agenda

How are asset prices formed when information is incomplete, noisy, and shaped by investor behavior?

My research agenda develops this question through a narrative that connects asset valuation, behavioral finance, Bayesian statistics, and data science. Rather than studying prices as purely mechanical outcomes, I examine how they emerge from the interaction between information, expectations, market structure, and uncertainty.

This page is designed as a visual and conceptual story. Each section can be connected to animations that show how signals enter the market, how investors react, how submarkets emerge, and how prices are formed through the interaction between signal, bias, volatility, and noise.

The Story of Price Formation

01

Market Price A Language with Its Own Grammar

April 2026

Imagine a person who wants to buy a stock for the first time in the financial market. Before doing so, several questions arise, but most of them revolve around two fundamental concerns, how much should I pay, and what is the best time to buy? Moreover, if we consider that the only observable feature of that asset is its current market price, we quickly realize that this information alone is not enough. Something more is needed to understand why that asset is priced the way it is at that moment.

This is, precisely, one of the most interesting problems in finance, particularly in the field of asset pricing. Understanding the value of an asset and determining how much one should pay for it is a task that occupies both professional and amateur investors. It is through their decisions that price movements are generated in the market. Taken together, these movements reflect a kind of imperfect consensus about the approximate value that an asset may have.

In a first approximation, we can think of the market price as a language that translates the opinions of the investors who participate in it. Each transaction, a purchase or a sale, is the result of an analysis, which can range from simple intuitions to highly sophisticated models, and which reflects the value that the asset could have at a given moment in time.

Perhaps the most interesting aspect of investing in stocks is not only choosing correctly, but learning to understand that language. But, as in any language, understanding it requires more than just listening; it requires knowing its grammar. Only in this way is it possible to correctly interpret what the market is saying and, more importantly, to express one’s own opinion clearly through investment decisions. In this process, the difference between gains and losses does not depend solely on the available information, but on the ability to read and participate in that language more precisely than others.

Once we understand that language, we can even decide with whom to interact. Because, as in any social circle, not everyone is talking about the same thing, nor do they share the same interests. Something similar happens in the market, some seek quick gains, others prefer to build value over the long term, and some simply react to what others are doing.

For this reason, the strategy we choose not only defines how we invest, but also which conversation we take part in within the market. In other words, it determines how we interpret information, whom we follow, consciously or unconsciously, and, ultimately, the way in which we aim to generate returns.

But then, what is the grammar behind the market price of a financial instrument? And what can it tell us about the strategy we want to follow in the stock market?

Let us imagine for a moment that we are in the middle of a conversation in a language we do not understand. At first, everything sounds the same; we only hear sounds. For us, it is pure noise. We are not able to identify words or give meaning to sentences. However, for those who understand that language, each phrase has structure, intention, and meaning.

Something similar happens in financial markets. At first glance, price movements may appear chaotic or incomprehensible. But financial theory acts as a guide that allows us to begin recognizing patterns, interpreting signals, and understanding what lies behind those changes. What previously seemed like noise begins to reveal information such as expectations, risks, and the decisions of other investors.

And here is where it becomes most interesting, although everyone observes the same price, not everyone interprets it in the same way. Each investor translates that language according to their information, their experience, and their objectives. For this reason, understanding the grammar of the market not only involves decoding what the price is saying, but also recognizing that its meaning is never entirely unique or definitive.

The grammar of the market is, in fact, simpler than it seems. The first component of an asset’s price is related to its ability to generate value over time. In the case of stocks, this is reflected in the productive processes of the firm and, ultimately, in the dividends it can deliver, as well as in its position within the market in which it operates.

This element constitutes the foundation of the entire financial sentence, because it allows us to understand why that asset exists and what sustains it over time. It is also the main reference for those who prefer long term relationships, since it allows us to infer how strong the relationship we will have with that asset may be, and above all whether it can be sustained over time. In other words, it is not only about how much it is worth today, but about how capable it is of continuing to generate value in the future.

The second element of this grammar is related to the expectations that are formed from the valuation of that asset. Although there are different ways to interpret information, this component is mainly associated with those investors who have greater access to information and who also have greater expertise in interpreting whether an asset is overvalued or undervalued.

This process allows the market, as a whole, to generate signals about possible price changes. These signals are later interpreted by smaller investors, who use them to make decisions about the path the price might take. Thus, expectations not only influence valuation directly, but also shape how information spreads across participants. This component remains just as important as the others.

Finally, there is noise. As noted by Fisher Black, noise is essential for price movements and liquidity. It is just as important as the previous components.

Noise reflects the opinions of a large number of investors, especially smaller ones. They agitate the market and allow the analyses of professional investors to make sense. Through noise, liquidity is provided to the market, and it becomes a space where multiple conversations occur in different directions.

It is essentially like entering a bar where many people are watching a game. Even if one can anticipate what the result might be, everyone has a different opinion about how the game will unfold.

This is where everything connects with strategy. If you are someone who enjoys emotion, the most interesting component for you may be noise, because it offers opportunities for short term gains. On the other hand, if you prefer a strategy that matures over time, you will likely focus on the first part of the sentence, the fundamentals. Meanwhile, expectations allow you to monitor the market and decide whether, in the short or long term, a change in strategy is necessary.

This means that two people can buy the same stock at the same moment and still have completely different interests. Their decisions will not be determined solely by the price, but by how they interpret the language of the market.

In the end, investing is not only about finding the correct price. It is about learning to read, interpret, and participate in a complex, dynamic, and deeply human language, a language in which each number tells a story, but whose true meaning depends on those who are able to listen beyond the noise.

02

Understanding price without fundamentals

April 2026

In traditional financial markets, price is often interpreted as an approximation of value. Under this logic, assets can be valued based on their future cash flows, and the market acts as a mechanism that aggregates information to reflect that value.

In cryptoassets, this idea breaks down. If an investor wants to participate in this market, a central question emerges. How should price formation be understood when there is no clear fundamental anchor. Are we facing an extension of traditional markets, or a completely different language.

One way to approach this question is to decompose price into three components, fundamental value, expectations, and noise. In traditional markets, this decomposition allows price to be interpreted as an approximation of intrinsic value. However, in the case of Bitcoin and other crypto-assets, this approach faces a clear limitation. These assets do not generate cash flows that can be discounted to obtain a valuation in the traditional sense.

This does not imply the absence of structure. Instead of being anchored in economic fundamentals, crypto-assets appear to depend on the dynamics of the market in which they are traded. Liquidity, adoption, interaction among assets, and aggregate market signals become central. While Bitcoin may behave as a relatively autonomous system, other crypto-assets depend more strongly on the broader ecosystem and their level of acceptance.

In this context, price is no longer an estimate of value. It becomes the result of a coordination process of expectations. It reflects what market participants believe others will be willing to pay in the future.

These expectations are not formed in isolation. They are built from signals such as past price movements, changes in volatility, trading volume, and market events. Each of these signals is incomplete, but together they allow investors to update their beliefs.

Noise also takes on a different role. It should not be understood only as random disturbance. In crypto-asset markets, noise contains information about participant heterogeneity, speculative intensity, and the speed at which expectations adjust. In this sense, noise is also a signal.

Inferring market price therefore does not mean uncovering a hidden value. It means understanding how expectations are coordinated in an environment of incomplete information. Price can be interpreted as a dynamic and probabilistic equilibrium between heterogeneous beliefs, partial signals, and continuous adjustment processes.

This structure did not emerge randomly. It has an origin. On September 15, 2008, Lehman Brothers declared bankruptcy, marking the beginning of a crisis that not only affected the financial system, but also fractured trust in its mechanisms.

For years, the system had promoted credit expansion under the assumption of stability. However, when borrowers began to default, the assets that supported that structure revealed their fragility. What appeared to be a robust system turned out to be highly vulnerable.

In that context, some actors began to explore alternatives. This is how Bitcoin emerged, not only as a technological innovation, but as a response to a deeper problem. The need to build a system in which trust does not depend on intermediaries, but on verifiable rules.

This new system introduced a different market structure. Unlike traditional assets, crypto-assets are traded across multiple independent exchanges, each with its own rules, levels of liquidity, and types of participants.

As a result, there is no single price at any given moment. The value of a crypto-asset may differ across platforms, reflecting local supply and demand conditions. However, through arbitrage, these differences tend to narrow, generating a dynamic convergence.

In this environment, price is not fixed. Price emerges, and because of this fragmented structure, adjustments can be extremely fast, which helps explain the high volatility observed in these markets.

In markets without clear fundamentals, price does not reveal an underlying truth. It reveals a process. A process in which information is fragmented, beliefs are heterogeneous, and uncertainty is continuously negotiated.

For this reason, in crypto-asset markets, volatility is not only a measure of risk. It is also a signal. A signal of how the market interprets, reacts to, and reshapes expectations in real time.

Understanding price, therefore, is not about finding its value, but about understanding the language in which it is formed.

Price Discovery for Crypto-Assets.
03

Prices emerge from signal, structure, and noise

Prices are not only reflections of fundamentals. They are also shaped by uncertainty, volatility, and the way investors coordinate their actions.

In this sense, noise is not simply a disturbance; it is part of the market process itself. A key objective of my work is to understand how market prices emerge from the interaction between meaningful signals, behavioral biases, and stochastic fluctuations.

04

Uncertainty requires probabilistic models

Because markets are uncertain, the modeling framework must represent uncertainty explicitly. This is why Bayesian statistics plays a central role in my research.

Bayesian methods allow the integration of prior knowledge, observed data, and latent market structures in a coherent way. Through hierarchical models, stochastic volatility frameworks, and Bayesian learning, I study how risk, heterogeneity, and information interact across assets and over time.

05

Data science helps reveal hidden structure

Data science complements financial theory by providing tools to detect nonlinear patterns, classify structures, and build predictive systems.

In my research, machine learning is not used as a substitute for economic reasoning, but as a bridge between theory, evidence, and decision-making. This allows me to connect pricing models with visual narratives that explain how markets learn, differentiate signal from noise, and generate observable price paths.
